# Five minutes on prefixprobe

## One sentence

If you can only run the first few steps of some of the tasks, `prefixprobe` tells you which prefixes to buy and how
close the resulting estimate of step rate and solve rate gets to a full run.

## 0:00 — The question

Each Multi-step SWE task chains 5–15 dependent engineering requests on one codebase
([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Klavis-AI/Multi-step-SWE/main/README.md)), and there are 69 tasks
([blog](https://www.klavis.ai/blog/multi-step-swe)). In the published results (`data/benchmark.js`) each of the nine
models has four runs of every task, except one task where Qwen has three, and the file carries a dollar cost per
model (`costData`). My inference, not a rule Klavis states: a new model placed the same way needs a comparable set of
runs, which is not cheap to repeat every time a model ships. The question: with a small fraction of that budget,
where should you spend it, and how wrong is the answer?

## 0:30 — Why the prefix is the unit

Step k of a task cannot run without steps 1..k-1. So the cost of a step is never the step: it is the prefix behind
it. That single fact rules out the usual "pick a random subset of items" answer — items are not independent, and they
are not equally priced. Probing the first k steps of a task also matches the published runs: they continue after a
failed step (every step of every run is scored), so a probe of a prefix is a truncation of a real run, not a
simulation of one.

## 1:00 — Two difficulty scales

For each step, the other eight models give two numbers. `d` is the log-odds of passing the step at all. `e` is the
log-odds of passing it *given a clean prefix*. The second one is the scale the solve rate lives on, because solving a
task is a chain of conditional passes.

## 1:30 — The estimator

One ability offset per model: pass probability is `sigmoid(a + d)`, with a normal prior pulled to zero, fitted by
Newton's method on the probe. Solving a task is then the product of `sigmoid(a_e + e)` over the steps after the probe.
That is textbook one-parameter item-response theory. I say so in the README: the statistics is not the contribution.

## 2:15 — The planner

The contribution is the spending rule for dependent items. Start at depth zero everywhere. A candidate extends one
task deeper; it costs the steps it adds and gains information, but the information of a task saturates at a rate set
by how much ability varies across tasks (`τ²`). So take the best feasible gain per unit cost until the budget is
gone. It is the greedy ratio rule for a precedence-constrained knapsack, with the saturation term added because
steps inside one task are not independent evidence.

## 3:00 — The evaluation

Hold one model out completely. Its fold — difficulties, costs, information, variance — is built from the other eight.
Its own results enter only through the probe. Score the estimate against the truth it never saw, for four budgets and
five strategies, then bootstrap over tasks (2000 replicates) to see how much of the result is just which tasks
happened to be drawn. Compare strategies pairwise within a replicate, because absolute error intervals shift upward
under resampling.

## 3:45 — The result

The generated table in the README is the answer: at each budget, the step-rate and solve-rate MAE per strategy, then
the paired greedy-minus-alternative difference with its interval, how many models improve and in what share of
replicates. I will read the numbers off the table rather than quote them from memory — they are regenerated by
`demo.py` and never typed by hand.

## 4:15 — What I would say against it

Nine folds is nine points; the bootstrap is over tasks, not models. The cost proxy is other models' agent time, not
dollars. Log-loss is computed on each plan's own unprobed steps and is therefore a calibration diagnostic, not a
ranking. Planning is fixed in advance rather than adaptive, and I designed the planner while looking at the same nine
models it is evaluated on.

## 4:45 — What would come next

Sequential planning (spend the next prefix based on what the first ones showed), a hierarchical fit instead of a
moment estimate for `τ²`, and per-step billing so the budget is measured in dollars.

## Questions you will get

**Isn't this just IRT?** The estimator is; the item structure is not. IRT assumes items you can pick independently.
Here items are chained and priced by their prefix, which is why the planner is a knapsack-style greedy over extensions
rather than a Fisher-information pick over items.

**Why not adaptive?** Because it is harder to evaluate honestly with nine models, and because a fixed plan can be
costed before it runs. It is listed as the next step, not as a limitation I am hiding.

**Why the prior?** A probe can be a handful of steps; the prior keeps a single lucky step from producing an ability
of five. It is weak enough not to matter once the probe is deep.

**Why percentage points?** Because a lab cares about being off by two points of step rate, not by a log-odds unit.
The log-odds model is internal; everything reported is on the scale people quote.
